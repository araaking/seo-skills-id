# 04 · Lowest Page Examples and Lessons

> Source: SQRG Sep 11, 2025, end of 4.6.7 and 4.7 (pp. 45–58). Rewritten and condensed in our own words.
> The full rules are in `03-pq-lowest.md`. Read this file when you need real cases for comparison. Indonesian counterpart: `references/id/04-pq-lowest-examples.md`.

**About the images here:** the images embedded in the example tables are only **rating sliders** (markers on the Lowest–Highest scale). Screenshots of the rated pages are **not embedded in the PDF**; each example title links to an external screenshot. The descriptions below are drawn from the explanatory text in the tables.

## End of 4.6.7: Lowest vs Low for copied content (p. 45)

- Sometimes content is clearly copied/paraphrased but the original can't be found (e.g., a Wikipedia article has changed a lot, or a summary was restructured). If you strongly suspect the page isn't the original, treat it as likely copied/paraphrased.
- If all/nearly all MC comes from elsewhere, judge **effort, originality and added value**:
  - **little or none** of the three → **Lowest**;
  - **low** → **Low**.

## Group A: Untrustworthy because of wrong content or deceptive purpose

**A1. Animal article with bizarre errors (p. 45)**
- Page: an informational article about the praying mantis.
- Problems: the animal's name suddenly changes to an unknown term, it mentions an animal that doesn't exist, oddly links animals to God, and claims venomous snakes don't exist (plainly false).
- Rating: untrustworthy, lowest E-E-A-T → **Lowest**. Probably auto-generated without human editing, but **how it was made doesn't matter**: the many strange factual errors are enough.
- Lesson: unverified AI content can drop straight to Lowest through factual errors, not because it's "AI".

**A2. Parenting blog that turns out to be an AI experiment (p. 46)**
- Page: an article on switching babies from breast milk to cow's milk. The footer says it's a blog helping parents make decisions.
- Problem: the terms of use say some articles are AI-generated for experimentation, meant for AI enthusiasts rather than the general public, and accuracy isn't guaranteed. No indication of which articles are AI-made.
- Rating: deceptive purpose (presented as a resource for parents) + lowest E-E-A-T → **Lowest**.
- Lesson: a disclaimer tucked on a separate page doesn't save you. Public claims on the page must match site policy.

**A3. Debt article pushing clicks (p. 48)**
- Page: an article suggesting clicking a link to avoid paying debts.
- Problems: advice contradicts expert consensus on debt relief; built to lure clicks on prominent links; debt-relief promises are a common scam pattern.
- Rating: financial YMYL, untrustworthy → **Lowest**.

**A4. Fake celebrity death "news" (p. 48)**
- A site imitating a news portal claims a famous singer has died.
- Problems: deliberately false; no info on who's responsible; no article date or date of death (basic elements of reporting).
- Rating: harmfully misleading + deceptive design → **Lowest**.

## Group B: Harmful

**B1. Racial supremacy site (p. 46)**
- Homepage of a site known for spreading white supremacy, antisemitism, Holocaust denial and Islamophobia.
- Rating: incites hatred against groups + worst reputation → **Lowest**. YMYL topic.

**B2. Pseudo-scientific group-superiority theory (p. 47)**
- A page promoting a group's superiority with baseless scientific claims about the role of melanin.
- Rating: harmful to groups + baseless claims → **Lowest**.

**B3. Advice on mixing drugs (p. 47)**
- A post encouraging mixing an anti-anxiety medication with a recreational drug.
- Rating: encourages dangerous behavior, contradicts expert consensus → **Lowest** (health YMYL).

**B4. Encouraging eating disorders (p. 47)**
- A page with posts urging unhealthy weight loss, part of a movement treating anorexia as a lifestyle.
- Rating: contradicts the consensus that anorexia is a life-threatening disorder → **Lowest**.

**B5. Eyewear seller threatening customers (p. 50)**
- Looks like an ordinary eyewear store, but major media and an encyclopedia document fraud and physical threats against customers who complained.
- Rating: worst reputation due to malicious behavior → **Lowest**.

## Group C: Data scams and suspicious stores

**C1. Fake payment form (p. 47)**
- Looks like a store payment page but asks for highly sensitive data: social security number, driver's license, bank account, ATM PIN.
- Rating: real purpose is identity/money theft → **Lowest**.

**C2. Branded sneakers store with minimal info (p. 49)**
- A product page for well-known branded basketball shoes.
- Problems: inadequate contact info for a store; the shipping and returns page names a seemingly unrelated company; official-looking logos of well-known organizations and payment services in the footer appear unaffiliated.
- Rating: looks potentially fraudulent → **Lowest**.
- Lesson: fake "trust" badges and inconsistent policies are scam signals.

**C3. Fake prize survey (p. 51)**
- A survey promising laptop/phone prizes, with a thank-you note from a domain resembling Google's name, that then asks for data like bank account numbers, with no info on who runs it.
- Rating: deceptive, scam traits → **Lowest**.

## Group D: YMYL without expertise or identity

**D1. Dental info with no identity (p. 49)**
- A medical site about a complication after tooth extraction, with no info on site owner, contact or author.
- Rating: medical pages need high trust; with zero identity → untrustworthy → **Lowest**.

**D2. Poorly written stomach-flu article (p. 50)**
- Poor writing, content inconsistent with standard medical guidance, author clearly lacks medical expertise. Flu causes many hospitalizations and deaths, so it's YMYL.
- Rating: **Lowest**.

**D3. Pimple-popping article (p. 50)**
- Extremely poor writing with almost no information; the opening sentence hedges without saying anything. No sign of skincare expertise.
- Rating: lowest E-E-A-T → **Lowest**.

**D4. Flat-earth site (p. 49)**
- Homepage of a community spreading flat-earth belief.
- Rating: baseless and against scientific consensus, undermining trust in scientific institutions (including the agency issuing severe-weather alerts) → **Lowest**.

**D5. Crystals cure cancer (p. 48)**
- An article claiming crystals can cure cancer, with a prominent crystal-shop link at the top.
- Rating: contradicts medical consensus; selling cancer "cures" is a common scam → **Lowest**.

## Group E: No MC or obscured MC

**E1. Chicken recipe list that is all ads (p. 50)**
- Looks like a recipe list, but every link is an ad.
- Rating: no MC + deceptive design → **Lowest**. Pages without MC crowd out quality content; ads disguised as MC are untrustworthy.

**E2. Cancer directory where every link is an ad (p. 51)**
- Looks like a list of resources on mesothelioma (a lung cancer), but every link is an ad.
- Rating: YMYL, no MC, deceptive design → **Lowest**.

**E3. Ads burying the MC (p. 51)**
- On arrival it seems to be only ads; the MC appears only after scrolling far down.
- Rating: many people would leave before finding the MC; MC deliberately obscured for the site's benefit → **Lowest**.

**E4. Credit report page with gibberish MC (p. 52)**
- Title suggests a government credit-report service, but the MC is meaningless text surrounded by ads/links. SC contains what look like a social friend request, a "you won" notice and a fake download button.
- Rating: extremely misleading title, no determinable purpose → **Lowest**.

## Group F: Spam, automation and copies

**F1. Organization page injected with drug spam (p. 52)**
- One page on a well-regarded green-building organization's site repeats a phrase about buying a prescription sedative over and over, plus a link.
- Rating: page created/altered to ride the site's quality and rank for that keyword → spam → **Lowest**.

**F2. Auto-generated car engine code page (p. 53)**
- A page about one engine error code, packed with SC, links and large ads. The code is repeated far beyond what's useful (irrelevant keywords).
- Problems: thin, unoriginal MC (code lists can be bought/scraped); the most important sentence, about the **cause** of the error, is blank because the template failed to fill; other pages on the site are also template-made without checking; no info on who's responsible.
- Rating: **Lowest**.
- Lesson: programmatic SEO without QA produces pages that are empty exactly where it matters.

**F3. Auto-generated "best bulldog wrinkle wipes" list (p. 54)**
- A product-recommendation page with affiliate links, auto-built from marketplace data by swapping product names into a generic template, with no editing or curation.
- Rating: its real purpose is to pull product searchers into affiliate clicks; **the affiliate disclosure doesn't help because there's no added value** → **Lowest**.

**F4. Copied erectile-dysfunction drug info site (p. 54)**
- All MC copied from elsewhere with no effort, expertise or curation; an "order now" link points to another, likely monetized site; no info on who's responsible.
- Rating: YMYL, **Lowest**.

**F5. Scaled content abuse: cancer ranking article (p. 55)**
- A YMYL medical article opening with a typical chatbot line about lacking real-time data and a knowledge cutoff, and ending mid-sentence.
- Rating: AI-made with no original content or value; spam + highly untrustworthy medical page → **Lowest**.
- Lesson: unedited leftover AI output is the most damning evidence.

**F6. Scaled content abuse: rabbit-breeder site writing about human tumors (p. 55)**
- Claims to be a community of rabbit lovers and breeders, but hosts hundreds of templated articles on unrelated topics, including human health (YMYL). MC is embedded from elsewhere or paraphrased.
- The Q&A format is a common scaled-content technique: collecting questions (e.g., from People Also Ask) and answering with paraphrased content, often via AI.
- Rating: deceptive about the site's purpose + scaled content abuse → **Lowest**.

**F7. Scaled content abuse: seniors site writing about chickens (p. 58)**
- Its About page describes non-medical info for seniors at home, yet it hosts hundreds of templated, paraphrased articles on anything, with embedded videos.
- Example page: an awkwardly phrased question about how much cold chickens can tolerate; the answer opens with an odd line, then a string of repeated questions whose answers make sense sentence by sentence but don't hang together.
- Rating: doesn't appear written or edited by a human → **Lowest**.
- Lesson: incoherent mass FAQ content is a scaled-content pattern.

## Group G: Domain and site reputation abuse

**G1. Expired domain abuse (p. 56)**
- A domain formerly owned by an organization concerned with rural education, now hosting Hong Kong lottery content. The Internet Archive shows its old look.
- Rating: highly misleading URL → **Lowest**. Visitors reasonably expect an education site.
- Lesson: URLs that look like government, school or charity sites but carry unrelated low-quality content (medical products, loans, lotteries, gambling) are misleading.

**G2. Site reputation abuse: sponsored supplement article on a news site (p. 57)**
- An article on a news site with a "Sponsored Content" banner and a disclosure that the byline belongs to paying advertisers, not the newsroom.
- Problems: content made by an outside party (the maker of the supplement) and placed to ride the outlet's reputation; despite the disclaimer it can be mistaken for newsroom journalism; it looks like an independent review but was written by the producer; clear conflict of interest.
- Rating: **Lowest**.
- Lesson: a "sponsored" label isn't enough if the main motive is riding the host's reputation and the content poses as an independent review.

## Recurring patterns across the examples

**[Application]**
1. **No info on who's responsible** shows up in nearly every spam and scam example.
2. **A site's description of itself not matching its content** (rabbit site writing about tumors; seniors site writing about chickens) signals scaled content.
3. **Templates without QA** show up as blank sections or sentences that don't connect.
4. **Disclosure (affiliate, sponsored, AI) doesn't save** pages that add no value or deceive.
5. **YMYL raises the stakes.** An error that might only be Low on a light topic can go straight to Lowest on health or finance.
