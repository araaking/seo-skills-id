## 1. Why people search and key definitions (12.0–12.1, pp. 94–95)

- People search from all kinds of devices and situations. Tasks can be simple (finding a film's director) or complex and multi-step (finding nearby showtimes, buying tickets, then getting directions). Search engines should make those tasks easy by surfacing helpful results right away.
- **User**: the person searching, from every country, language, age, gender, race, religion and political affiliation.
- **Query**: text, image or other input given to a search engine: typed, spoken, pasted, from a camera/another app, or from "related searches". Written in square brackets in the guidelines, e.g., [coffee shops near me].
- **Query interpretation**: possible meanings (e.g., [apple] as the computer brand or the fruit).
- **User intent**: what the user wants to accomplish. One interpretation can carry many intents (a film: showtimes, reviews, cast), and one person may have several at once.
- **Locale**: the task's language and region, written with a two-letter country code (e.g., "English (US)"; for Indonesia: "Indonesian (ID)").
- **User location**: a more specific location (city/state), sometimes shown on a map. Crucial for nearby searches, irrelevant for universal questions like how gravity works.
- **SERP**: the results page, made up of **result blocks**. **Result** = result block + landing page. **Landing page (LP)** = the page opened after clicking a link in the block.

## 2. Understanding the query (12.2, p. 95)

- Understanding the query is step one. If unclear, research it (Google, dictionaries, encyclopedias).
- **Don't rely on Google's top results.** A query may have meanings not reflected on the SERP. Don't rate a page highly just because it ranks at the top.
- Imagine users in the locale typing/speaking the query on their phone. Examples: [population of paris] → Paris's current population; [starbucks near me] → the nearest outlet; [weather] → weather at the user's location right now.

## 3. Locale and user location (12.3, p. 96)

- Users in different locales can expect different things from the same query.
- **Illustration in the PDF (p. 96):** two stick figures with thought bubbles. The query [football] in English (UK) brings a soccer ball to mind; in English (US), an American football.
- For many queries, user location doesn't change the meaning (e.g., a specific social network, kitten pictures, the Earth–Moon distance).
- Test question: **would someone in a different city or country want something else?**

## 4. Queries with an explicit location (12.4, p. 97)

- People often put the desired location right in the query, regardless of where they are. An **explicit location** like this makes the query far clearer.
- **Illustration in the PDF (p. 97):** two users both located in Dallas. One searches [Dallas hotels] because their mother is visiting (explicit location = user location). The other searches [New York hotels] for a trip (explicit location differs from user location).
- **Give priority to an explicit location in the query.**

## 5. Queries with multiple meanings (12.5, pp. 98–100)

| Interpretation type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Dominant** | What most users mean. Not every query has one |
| **Common** | What many or some users mean. There can be several |
| **Minor – reasonable** | Meant by fewer people, but still useful to include since needs vary |
| **Minor – unlikely** | Possible in theory, but very few mean it |
| **No chance** | Almost nobody means it; results feel off-topic even if words match (e.g., an overheated pet for [hot dog]) |

**Diagram in the PDF (p. 99)**, where the number of person icons shows the share of users:
- [apple] (US): the computer company = **dominant** (most icons); the fruit (and a person's name) = **common**; a city name = **unlikely minor**.
- [mercury] (US): the planet and the chemical element = **common**; an insurance company and a marine engine brand = **reasonable minor**; a British WWII aircraft = **unlikely minor**.

**Already-specific queries** leave few possible meanings (table on p. 100):
- A county's **non-emergency** phone number: an emergency number isn't a reasonable intent. **Mind the important words in the query**; users get frustrated when they're ignored.
- Whether egg salad is healthy: users want health info (calories, fat, ingredient nutrition). Egg salad recipes without nutrition info aren't a reasonable minor intent, even if they might be a next step.
- Whether bears can climb mountains: a Know Simple question about bears' ability. Climbing info for a national monument whose name contains "bears" isn't a reasonable intent.
- **Matching a few keywords doesn't make a result relevant or helpful.**

## 6. Query meanings change over time (12.6, p. 101)

- Assume users want **current info**, the latest product model, or the latest instance of a recurring event, unless the query says otherwise.
- **Illustration in the PDF (p. 101):** a US president's surname meant the 41st president to a user in 1994 but the 43rd to a user in 2004. [iphone] in 2007 meant the first iPhone; in 2022, the latest model at that time. The dominant interpretation keeps shifting.

## 7. Intent types (12.7, pp. 101–107)

Four broad categories (boundaries can blur; one query can fit more than one):

### 7.1 Know and Know Simple (12.7.1, pp. 102–103)
- **Know**: finding information or exploring a topic.
- **Know Simple**: a Know subtype seeking a very specific answer (a fact, a diagram) that is **correct, complete and fits in a small space**. Rule of thumb: most people agree on the answer, and it can be given in a sentence or two or a brief list. It needn't be phrased as a question (someone's height can be searched with or without a question word).
- **Not Know Simple:** broad/complex/deep queries; ambiguous ones; controversial topics; no single right answer; different users needing different info or sources; exploring; seeking inspiration/ideas; seeking opinions and perspectives of real people.

| Know Simple | Know (broader) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A former president's height | The former president's name alone | People may want a biography, books, social media |
| A big city's population in a given year | The city name alone | Could be travel info, facts, photos, history |
| Whom a disease is named after | The disease name alone | Varied medical needs, no single answer |
| A department store's opening hours | Gift-wrapping options at that store | A specific service with no short answer |
| The chemical symbol for nickel | What nickel is used for | No short, complete answer |
| Winner of a championship final in a given year | Who will win | Opinion, no definite answer |
| A company's share price | Whether to buy that stock | Yes/no in theory, but no answer everyone agrees on |
| [weather], [weather today], etc. | How people predict weather | [weather] has a simple need: temperature and chance of rain/snow. The second is broad |
| What a particular haircut is | Hairstyles for men with round faces | Seeking inspiration, no definite answer |
| Social security retirement age | Retirement planning | Complex and situational; different people need different info |

### 7.2 Do (12.7.2, p. 103)
- Wanting to **accomplish a goal or do an activity**: download, buy, obtain, be entertained, or interact with a site/app. Can be immediate, or open-ended with several rounds of browsing.
- Examples: getting a high-school equivalency diploma online; an online personality test; calculating BMI; paying a parking ticket; watching a specific series; watching a specific music video; [music videos] (not specific, but still wants to watch after choosing); ideas for organizing a small bathroom (an open-ended Do: some want images/video for inspiration, some want guides, some both).

### 7.3 Website (12.7.3, p. 104)
- Wanting to open a **specific site or page** (the *target*).
- **URL queries**: a perfect, working URL (with or without http/www), or an imperfect URL that won't load but clearly points to a page.
- Examples: a big forum's login page; a short-video app's site; a portal's email service; a big retailer's site; a specific wheelchair product page on its maker's site.

### 7.4 Visit-in-Person and user location (12.7.4, pp. 105–107)
- Looking for info in order to **go somewhere**: coffee shops, gas stations, ATMs, restaurants.
- **Illustrations in the PDF (pp. 105–107):** stick figures with thought bubbles. Someone needing a car repair shop or a gas station "right now" = Visit-in-Person. Someone asking Mount Everest's height or wanting an online IQ test = not.
  - Other Visit-in-Person: pizza, yoga classes, grocery stores, movie showtimes, car repair, dentists, a bank's ATM locations, nearby libraries.
  - Not Visit-in-Person: sports teams, word-game cheats, word definitions, stock tickers, singers, games, small dog breeds, dance videos, award shows, jokes, a bank's login page.
- **Could be both:** a big retail chain's name (go to the nearest store **or** shop online), hotels, post offices, fashion brands, libraries, bank names.
- **Location can change meaning:** near Austin, Texas, [verbena] could mean a popular restaurant called Verbena or the verbena plant. Elsewhere, with no place by that name, there's only one meaning: the plant.

## 8. Queries with multiple intents (12.7.5, p. 107)

- Many queries have more than one reasonable intent. Given how many people search for how many different things, **lean toward treating interpretations and intents as reasonable**, and research when needed.
- Example: a famous university's name → official homepage (Website), directions (Visit-in-Person), admissions/history/news/sports (Know). But one specific course at that university is an unreasonable intent for such a short query.
- Example: a big retail chain's name → nearest store (Visit-in-Person), homepage to shop online (Website), company info/news/social (Know). But news about the company's charitable donations from 20-odd years ago is an unreasonable intent.

## 9. Result blocks (12.8, pp. 108–112)

**Web Search Result Block (12.8.1):** a linked title, a URL and a text *snippet*. For many queries, the most helpful result type.
- Examples in the PDF: a query for reviews of a food-processor brand (Know: wants recent reviews) with a block linking to a review page; a query for Broadway tickets (Know or Do: check prices and/or buy).

**Special Content Result Block / SCRB (12.8.2):** appears on the SERP alongside web results and shows content directly on the SERP, from calculators and playable videos to interactive weather. Some also link to a landing page.
- Raters assume interactive features **work normally** (tasks only show screenshots).
- Very time-sensitive SCRB info (stocks, weather) may be stale by rating time; **don't penalize it**, assume it shows current info.
- **Screenshot examples in the PDF (pp. 109–112):** a weather block for a user in a city (Know Simple); a swipeable block of an actress's films with per-film links (Know); a calories-in-a-fruit block (Know Simple); a latest league scores block (Know); a playable music-video block with artist/album info (Do); a "tallest tree" answer block with a collage of redwood photos, a short answer paragraph and a source article link (Know Simple).

## 10. Implications for keyword research and content

**[Application]**
- **Classify every target keyword as Know / Know Simple / Do / Website / Visit-in-Person** before writing. Format follows intent: Know Simple → direct answer up front; Know → comprehensive article; Do → a tool, steps or a clear CTA; Visit-in-Person → location info, hours, map.
- **Don't chase Know Simple with long articles.** A short, correct, complete answer at the top fits better; the rest is extra context.
- **Map interpretations:** dominant, common, reasonable minor. Articles for ambiguous queries should focus on the dominant interpretation or clearly say which one they cover.
- **Don't ignore important words in the keyword** (e.g., "non-emergency", "without", "for beginners", "2026"). Writing about a similar topic while ignoring them = irrelevant content.
- **Freshness:** for products, regulations and recurring events, users expect the latest version. Update articles when new versions or rules come out.
- **Indonesian context (examples):** [bola] usually means football (soccer); [cuaca] means weather at the user's location; [samsat terdekat] (nearest vehicle registration office) is Visit-in-Person; a marketplace or bank name can mean Website (login/app) and Visit-in-Person (branch offices) at once.
- **Intent research shouldn't rely on Google's SERP alone.** Also look at real user questions (forums, social media, comment sections) to catch reasonable minor intents.
