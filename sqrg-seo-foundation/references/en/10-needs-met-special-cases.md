## 1. How Page Quality and Needs Met relate (14.0, pp. 142–143)

- **Needs Met depends on the query and the result.** **Page Quality does not depend on the query**; PQ is judged on the landing page alone.
- Not every result has a PQ slider. If it does, rate the landing page.
- **HM isn't appropriate** for pages that are untrustworthy or have other bad traits (outdated or inaccurate info). The HM bar is very high.
- Pages of such low PQ that they're unhelpful or harmful (gibberish, scams) → **FailsM**, because the PQ problems block helpfulness. **Exception:** queries with clear website intent; the target site is still **FullyM**, since people may search for any site.
- **Useless results are always FailsM, even with a high-PQ landing page.** Useless is useless.

**Examples (p. 143):**
- Dehydration symptoms: an on-topic but untrustworthy, potentially misleading article → low NM, low PQ (YMYL with no expertise). A page on a highly authoritative medical site → very helpful NM, very trustworthy PQ.
- Octaves on a guitar: a block showing the octave count of a **piano**, linking to a good piano page → **FailsM** for NM, while the landing page's PQ is Medium+ to High. **High PQ doesn't rescue an answer in the wrong context.**

## 2. Flags (15.0–15.6, pp. 144–151)

Flags are judged on **the result alone**, independent of the query.

**Porn (15.1–15.2):**
- Flag every porn page whatever the query (including prominent porn ads). Cultural standards can differ by locale.
- Queries clearly **not** seeking porn (common words about women, family, toys, car pictures, questions about animals) → a porn result = **FailsM**. Unrequested porn is a very bad experience.
- Queries with a **possible** porn meaning (e.g., anatomical terms) → rate as if the non-porn meaning is dominant (e.g., health/anatomy info).
- Queries **clearly** seeking porn → still rate helpfulness. Porn results aren't automatically MM/HM; pages trying to install malware still rate low.

**Reporting illegal images (15.3):** the internal procedure for raters to report child sexual abuse material. Not relevant to SEO; not summarized here.

**Foreign Language (15.4):**
- **Not** used if the landing page's language is the task language, a language widely spoken by people in the task location, or English. Example: for Catalan tasks in Spain, Catalan, Spanish and English pages get no flag.
- Used even if the rater personally understands the language, when most users in the locale don't. Based on the landing page's language, not the block's appearance. When in doubt, don't flag.
- Foreign-language pages are **usually FailsM** because they can't be understood. **Unless** the query clearly wants one, e.g., a US user searching for a Chinese search engine's site → FullyM + flag.
- **Videos** often still help in a foreign language: a requested song, film or match can be enjoyed without understanding the language. But a video of someone **talking about** that song/film in a foreign language → FailsM + flag.
- Examples: a video of a Canadian singer performing a French song, on a page mostly in English → exactly what's wanted, no flag. A clip from a Filipino film in Tagalog → helpful to many US users searching for it.

**Did Not Load (15.5):**
- Used only when the landing page shows a server/application error with no other content (no navigation, home link, SC or ads), or is completely blank. Based on the landing page, not the block.
- **Not** used for: malware warnings, removed or expired content (lapsed classifieds, deleted posts, unavailable products), or content needing a subscription.
- Pages that truly don't load → FailsM. Customized error pages are part of a well-run site and **can** still help.

**Examples (p. 150):**
- Kids' snow shoes in a specific color → a reputable store's error page with lots of SC, a search box, tips and customer service info: NM unhelpful (no info on that product), PQ still good. No flag.
- Scripture passages → a "no results" page on a well-known reference site that still links to every passage by book, plus search and tips: **very helpful**. No flag.
- Theater tickets → a malware warning: FailsM and PQ Lowest. No Did Not Load flag.
- A horror film → server not found, no MC/SC/ads: Did Not Load flag, FailsM, no PQ.

**Additional flags (15.6):** some tasks ask for **Upsetting-Offensive** (content that could upset or offend a typical user in the locale) and **Not-for-Everyone** (content unsuitable for public spaces, workplaces or schools).

## 3. Many kinds of helpful results (16.0, p. 152)

- Very helpful results deserve HM whatever reasonable interpretation or intent they serve, minor ones included. Don't assume; research the query.
- Even within one interpretation, different people have different intents (e.g., for a film: reviews, social chatter, cast, where to watch), and one person may have all of them.
- Diverse results help, even for queries with one clear intent: different **formats** (text, video, images, audio), different **experiences and opinions** (personal blogs, social posts), different **depths** (a 10-second summary video vs a one-hour documentary), different **site types** (corporate, nonprofit, government, social, forums, personal blogs).
- Queries most in need of diversity: experience/opinion seeking (should I get a dog), subjective (best running music), exploration/browsing (bathroom remodel ideas, a black suit, affordable engagement rings), and no single right answer (how to ask for a raise).

## 4. Website plus visit-in-person intent (16.1, pp. 152–154)

- Two strong intents: (1) go to the site (find info, buy online, book, make an appointment, customer service); (2) visit in person.
- **Results meeting only one intent must not be FullyM.**
- Examples: a big retailer, a vehicle-licensing office, or a bank name, with a user location → a block of nearby locations serves the visit intent; the official site serves online intents (shopping, renewing a license, paying fees, online banking). Both are very satisfying; neither is FullyM.

## 5. Query vs landing page specificity (17.0, pp. 154–157)

- Queries range from general to specific (chair → dining chair → one model from a particular furniture brand). Specific queries are easier to rate. **Rate by helpfulness, not by how well the specificity matches.**
- For broad categories (cafés, restaurants, hotels, books, tourist sights), **popular and prominent examples** can count as very helpful.

**Examples:**
- [credit cards] → a popular card company's page can be very helpful despite being more specific (PQ High+ to Highest); a top-cards list from a consumer-finance site recommended by major media fits most users (PQ High); a card requiring union membership = unlikely minor interpretation, even with High PQ.
- [hotels] → a hotel page on a popular travel aggregator = very helpful; a big hotel chain's homepage = can be very helpful; the page of **one** specific branch = too specific, less helpful (PQ still high).
- A big retailer's name → its store-locator page = very helpful for visitors (and the most authoritative page on store locations); a page for one type of women's pants on that retailer's site = unlikely to no-chance interpretation.

## 6. Freshness (18.0, pp. 158–160)

| Query type | Examples | Assumption |
|---|---|---|
| Breaking news | Tornado, tsunami | Info is needed right now |
| Recurring events | Olympics, talent shows, team schedules, tax forms, elections | The latest or upcoming edition |
| Current info | City population, national debt, airfares, next public holiday | The most recent figure or info |
| Products | Phones, car models, operating systems | The latest model/version |

- For queries needing new content, **only current or updated pages** deserve high NM. Old pages, old models/prices or outdated info are **stale** → low NM, sometimes FailsM.
- Some queries carry both news and "timeless" intents (e.g., a celebrity or politician: biography or latest gossip).
- **Freshness generally matters less for PQ.** Archives of reputable outlets can have high PQ. But abandoned sites or unmaintained content that becomes inaccurate/misleading → low PQ.
- Publication dates can differ from update dates. Some sites always show today's date. The Wayback Machine can help reveal a page's history.

**Examples:**
- Traffic in a big city: today's conditions = helpful; yesterday's = **stale and useless**, even at one day old.
- Nobel Peace Prize winner: a page on the latest winner = helpful; on a winner from several years earlier = stale (very minor interpretation).
- Arthritis: an authoritative, regularly updated medical site = helpful; a medical news article from over a decade ago = very stale, **potentially dangerous**.
- A past president's birth date: no fresh results needed; the fact doesn't change.

## 7. Misspelled queries and name queries (19.0–19.2, pp. 160–161)

- **Obvious** misspellings (a logistics company, a sports team or a singer spelled wrong) → rate by the real intent, not the spelling.
- **Not obviously** misspelled → respect the query as written.
- **Names:** don't assume a typo. Example: a name resembling a famous comedian's but spelled differently is taken as a search for people with exactly that name, since many people have it.
- Example: a legendary athlete's name with an unusual first-name spelling → a professional network page listing all members with that spelling = very helpful. Results about the famous athlete count as an unlikely minor interpretation and are somewhat helpful. Results for more prominent people with that name rate higher than for less prominent ones.

## 8. URL queries that needn't be Fully Meets (20.0, pp. 161–162)

- For a valid URL query, ratings aren't limited to FullyM or FailsM. Looking for **info about the site** (reviews, news) is a reasonable intent; raters even use this for reputation research.
- Results with reviews and reputation info can be very helpful. **Traffic-statistics sites usually aren't.**
- Examples: a home-furnishing store's URL → the store's site is the target; that store's coupon codes on a well-known coupon site = very helpful, especially for repeat shoppers; reviews of the store on a well-known review site = helpful for new shoppers. A professor-rating site's URL → the site is the target; a thorough encyclopedia article about it (how ratings work, how they compare with classroom evaluations, and criticisms) = very helpful.

## 9. Product queries: research and browsing (21.0, pp. 163–164)

- Some are clearly Know (product reviews), clearly Do (buy a product), clearly Website (product + official store). **But most product queries don't state their intent.**
- Many people enjoy **browsing** products online like window-shopping. **Give high NM to results that support research, browsing and deciding.** For big purchases (cars, major appliances), buying is often preceded by research (reviews, specs), understanding options (brands, models, prices) and comparing.
- **PQ for product results needs extra care.**
- Examples: HD TV with no size/brand → a page explaining TV types plus reviews of many models from a site known for electronics reviews. Toddler jeans in a specific size (specific) → a quality jeans store page with many options in that size. Cufflinks → a large collection from a reputable retailer with style and price filters. A specific ring from a jewelry brand → image results of the ring from many angles and materials.

## 10. Visit-in-person: how near is "near" (22.0–22.1, pp. 164–166)

- If the query names no location and a user location exists, results **at or near the user's location** help most.
- "Near" depends on the place: gas stations, coffee shops, supermarkets → people won't travel far. Doctors, libraries, specific restaurant types, public pools, hiking trails → a bit farther is fine. Highly specialized clinics → can be far.
- **Location matters:** minimum wage, user in a US state → a block with the correct figure for that state (accuracy must be verified) and that state's official page = very helpful; a national page with every state's rate = very helpful though it takes effort; another state's official page = unhelpful.
- **Location doesn't matter:** jobs at a big tech company → a block with no job info = FailsM; the company's official careers page = FullyM, wherever the user is.

## 11. English results in non-English locales (23.0–23.1, pp. 167–172)

**Principles:**
- NM reflects helpfulness for users **in that locale**. If the query is in the locale's language, **assume users want results in that language**.
- English results deserve high NM only if users in the locale expect them. Unless the query asks for them, English results are treated as **useless** if most users can't read them.
- Each locale has its own considerations: official and regional languages, dialects, scripts and keyboard languages. **When in doubt, assume users prefer the task language** unless the query clearly signals otherwise.

**Examples (Hindi–India and Korean locales):**
- A person's name in local script → a local-language encyclopedia article is very helpful; the English version with similar content helps little because few can read it.
- **Proper nouns typed in Latin script** (a famous footballer, a film title + year) → users still want local-language results (local articles, local reviews and stores).
- **Mixed queries** (local language in Latin script with some English words, e.g., a women's cricket match; or a brand + "tablet") → still want local-language results, e.g., the brand's product page in the local language.
- **When English satisfies:** the official site of a global organization that's being sought (e.g., "famous university website" typed in local script → its English official site fully satisfies); technical info usually expressed in English (part numbers, specs, formulas). Example: a Korean-language tutorial on restoring a network switch's operating system, with the exact console commands in English → very helpful.
- In locales where English is official or dominant (e.g., Singapore), English results can be very helpful.

## 12. Dictionary and encyclopedia results (24.0, pp. 172–174)

- Dictionaries and encyclopedias are often topically relevant but **don't help for common words most people already understand**. High NM only when the intent is to learn what something is or what a word means.
- For very common words, a dictionary definition may only be **SM**.
- Examples: a hard vocabulary word common in college entrance exams → a dictionary result is very helpful. "Rainbow" → most people know it; images or a simple description help more than a dictionary. "Cafeteria" with a user location → most want a place to eat; even an in-depth encyclopedia article isn't very helpful. History of ATMs → an encyclopedia article with a thorough history section is very helpful. ATM near me → an encyclopedia article is **FailsM**, with no location info.

## 13. Implications for content creators

**[Application]**
- **On-topic but wrong-context content still fails.** Answer exactly what's asked (guitar, not piano; the requested year, not another).
- **Write in Indonesian for Indonesian queries**, including queries with proper nouns or English terms in Latin script (e.g., brand + "review", film titles, names). Technical terms, commands, code, part numbers and specs can stay in English; that's what users look for.
- **Regional and mixed languages:** for Indonesian audiences, Bahasa Indonesia is the safest default. If targeting readers of a specific regional language, match their needs.
- **Product and category pages:** support browsing and research (filters, multi-angle photos, specs, comparisons, reviews), not just a buy button.
- **Broad category pages** (e.g., "hotels in Bali", "cafés in South Jakarta"): show popular, prominent examples, not one overly specific place.
- **Local content:** figures that vary by region (regional minimum wages UMR/UMP, fees, local rules) must be region-specific, accurate, and cite that region's official source.
- **Freshness:** content on recurring events (elections, state university entrance exams, civil-service CPNS recruitment, holiday schedules), prices and product models must be updated. For medical topics, update to the latest guidelines; outdated medical content can be dangerous.
- **Error and "out of stock" pages:** provide navigation, search and alternatives. Helpful error pages still have value.
- **Definition articles:** only worth making if the term's meaning is actually searched for. For common words, the intent is usually something else (places, products, images).
- **Your own brand:** make sure pages serve both intents (location/branch info and online services), and monitor reviews and reputation that surface for your domain name.
