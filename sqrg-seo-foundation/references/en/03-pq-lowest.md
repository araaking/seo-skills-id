## 1. Initial screening and Lowest triggers (4.0, pp. 29–30)

**Steps 1 and 2** of PQ rating (check purpose, check potential for harm) act as a **quick filter** before other quality factors are considered.

**Initial considerations:**
- Harmful purpose, or deceptive about its purpose/who's responsible → Lowest is required.
- MC harmful to self/others, harmful to specified groups, or harmfully misleading → Lowest is required.
- Any topic or site type can be Lowest. Extra scrutiny for those needing high trust: online stores, medical sites, coverage of major civic issues.
- **A page made to benefit the owner (e.g., to earn money) while making almost no effort to help visitors → Lowest is required.**

**Any one of these justifies Lowest (table, p. 30):**

| Aspect | Lowest trigger |
|---|---|
| Site/creator info | Zero information on who runs the site or answers for its content, on YMYL pages or pages needing trust |
| Reputation | Severely negative reputation, e.g., known for malicious or harmful conduct |
| E-E-A-T | Page/site is highly untrustworthy; lack of E-E-A-T makes the page fail its purpose |
| Ads and SC | MC deliberately blocked or hidden by ads, SC, interstitials, download links, or anything else that serves the owner rather than the visitor |
| Title | Extremely misleading, shocking or exaggerated title |
| MC quality | Hacked, defaced or spam-filled page; gibberish; MC made with little or no effort or originality and **no added value versus similar pages on the web**; MC so low in effort/originality/skill that the page fails its purpose |

## 2. The three Lowest families (4.1, p. 31)

| Family | Type | Short examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Harmful** | Harmful to self/others (4.2) | Detailed suicide instructions; serious instructions to kill someone |
| | Harmful to specified groups (4.3) | Encouraging violence or mistreatment of a group; deeply insulting/dehumanizing stereotypes |
| | Harmfully misleading info (4.4) | Harmful false info easily refuted by common facts; harmful baseless theories/claims |
| **Untrustworthy** | Untrustworthy pages/sites (4.5) | Deceptive purpose or design; manipulating people into actions that benefit the site while causing harm |
| **Spammy** | Spam pages (4.6), also per Google Search Essentials spam policies | Deliberately no MC or gibberish MC; hacked/defaced/spammed pages |

Categories can overlap (harmfully misleading info is also untrustworthy).

**Important:** lots of content is controversial, one-sided, unpleasant or disliked by some people yet does **not** meet the Lowest criteria. Use the guideline standards, not personal opinion.

## 3. Harmful to self or others (4.2, p. 32)

**Lowest if** content promotes, shows, stirs up or directly inflicts harm, whether physical, mental, emotional or financial. This includes direct attempts to hurt people, encouraging risky behavior, extreme violence/gore with no educational purpose, or severely traumatic content.

- **The yardstick is the most vulnerable person.** It needn't harm everyone. If there's a reasonable chance it harms the most vulnerable (to scams, unaware of stunt dangers, unable to handle violent content), treat it as harmful.
- **Exceptions:** beneficial content that reports on, discusses or educates about harmful things (fiction, credible news, education, advocacy such as a page about a protest against domestic violence).

| Counts as harmful | Does not |
|---|---|
| Inciting violence; serious/realistic death threats | Violence in an action movie |
| Exposing personal data to target or harass someone (doxxing) | News about violent events |
| Easy-to-copy instructions for violent acts | Educational content that may show violence or gross imagery |
| Glorifying or trivializing violence/atrocities, disparaging victims | Explaining scams to raise awareness |
| Facilitating serious harm, or justifying abuse of people | Dangerous stunts shown with risks, expertise and equipment explained so people don't copy them |
| Encouraging unsafe behavior or grossly downplaying risk (e.g., consuming cleaning products) | |
| Promoting suicide; pro-anorexia content | |
| Health advice contradicting expert consensus that could cause serious harm or delay life-saving treatment | |

## 4. Harmful to specified groups (4.3, p. 33)

**Lowest if** it spreads, excuses or stirs up hatred toward a **Specified Group**: groups defined by age, caste, disability, ethnicity, gender identity and expression, immigration status, nationality, race, religion, sex/gender, sexual orientation, veteran status, victims (and relatives of victims) of large-scale violent events, and any other trait tied to systemic discrimination or marginalization (e.g., refugees, homeless people).

- The tone must be **serious** (not joking/satire) or **mean-spirited** (meant to demean or spread intolerance). Comedy or art isn't harmful unless clearly mean-spirited.
- **Criticizing objects, philosophies or ideas** generally isn't an attack on a group. Criticizing a religious doctrine isn't the same as attacking its followers.
- Educational content (definitions, research), news and historical documents aren't considered harmful.

| Counts as harmful | Does not |
|---|---|
| Encouraging violence or mistreatment of a group | A WWII history documentary featuring speeches by Nazi leaders |
| Showing a staunch refusal to tolerate a group's beliefs/behavior | Stand-up comedy that plays on stereotypes without malice |
| Implying one group is superior/inferior to another | A newspaper article about a hate organization |
| Deeply insulting or dehumanizing stereotypes (stereotypes can be negative or positive) | A dictionary definition of a slur |
| | A discussion of a religious text's views on women |

## 5. Harmfully misleading information (4.4, pp. 34–35)

**Lowest if** it misleads in ways that can harm people and society, whether deliberately or because the creator truly believes it. Accuracy standards are highest for clear YMYL topics.

| Type | Trait | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Clearly false and harmful | Refutable by simple, widely accepted facts | False claim that a world leader died; wrong election date |
| Harmful claims against expert consensus | Contradicts positions/findings agreed by authorities in the field | Lemons cure cancer; lottery tickets as a guaranteed retirement plan |
| Harmful baseless theories/claims | Not grounded in reasonable facts/evidence, especially those eroding trust in public institutions; includes thoroughly debunked or outlandish ones | Claims the US government orchestrated the 9/11 attacks; world leaders are lizard people |

- It can come from sources that look "expert", "authoritative" or "official". **The source doesn't save it.**
- The judgment can rest on the MC **or** other page traits: e.g., a harmfully misleading **title** even if the body isn't, or a creator faking medical credentials in a health video.
- **Not included** (if the page only contains these): non-YMYL entertainment with no factual claims (fiction, satire, parody, astrology, folklore, myths, urban legends); reviews of personal taste/opinion; reasonably debatable matters with no single right answer (e.g., the effectiveness of different healthcare systems); minor errors on trivial topics (e.g., a celebrity's height).
- **Persuasive, one-sided or polarizing content** falls here only if it can harm individuals/groups **and** is clearly false, contradicts expert consensus, or is baseless.
- How to check: look for trustworthy sources and expert consensus as time allows. Fact-checkers can't debunk everything. Very implausible claims that independent trustworthy sources can't verify → treat as baseless.

## 6. Untrustworthy pages or sites (4.5, pp. 35–38)

**Lowest if** the rater strongly suspects deceptive or malicious practices. Judgment can rest on page content, site/creator info, reputation, **or missing critical info** (sites handling transactions or sensitive data must make clear who's responsible and how to contact them).

- One odd page on an unknown site isn't necessarily a problem. But a **pattern** of deception/manipulation, or feeling unsafe → Lowest, and leave.
- **No need to "prove" harm.** A highly untrustworthy page fails its purpose because people won't use it.

**Traits of untrustworthy pages:**
- Many or major factual errors on an informational page, so people lose trust.
- Inadequate site/creator info for the purpose.
- Lowest-level E-E-A-T or reputation.
- Deceptive purpose, design or intent.
- MC deliberately blocked/hidden.
- Signs of fraud, malware downloads or other harmful conduct.
- Designed to manipulate people into acting for the site's benefit while harming themselves, others or groups.

### 6.1 Inadequate site/creator info (4.5.1, pp. 35–36)
- For pages needing high trust, info on who made and who's responsible for the content is **critical**.
- Many scam and malicious-download pages deliberately show no or fake info so victims have no recourse.
- Sites handling personal/sensitive data (passwords, personal info, transactions) **must** have extensive contact info.
- **YMYL pages or sites handling sensitive data with no info at all about the site/creator → Lowest.**

### 6.2 Lowest E-E-A-T and reputation (4.5.2, p. 36)
- If E-E-A-T is so low that people can't/shouldn't use the MC → Lowest. **A highly inexpert YMYL page = untrustworthy → Lowest.**
- Reputation so negative that many people would consider the site untrustworthy → Lowest for all its pages. Examples carried over from 3.3: the children's play-equipment store with fraud evidence, the eyewear seller with criminal behavior, and the veterans organization with reported fund mismanagement.

### 6.3 Deception: purpose, site info, design (4.5.3, pp. 36–38)

Any form of deception → Lowest. Three kinds:
- **Deceptive purpose:** looks like it has one purpose but exists for another.
- **Deceptive info about the site/creator:** deliberately inaccurate to appear trustworthy.
- **Deceptive design:** deliberately made to look like one kind of page while functioning as another.

**Deceptive purpose** examples:
- Product recommendations with deliberately false info to earn clicks on monetized links, e.g., posing as a celebrity blog, or **claiming independent testing that never happened**.
- Posing as an independent review while existing only to make money, with misleading info to push affiliate clicks or purchases.

**Deceptive site/creator info** examples:
- Impersonating another site (copied logo/branding, look-alike URL).
- A fake (non-satirical) social profile impersonating someone.
- Looks like a news/information site, but its articles manipulate readers for political, financial or other gain.
- Posing as a celebrity's personal site without permission, with fake testimonials to drive clicks/purchases.
- Fake business info, e.g., claiming a physical store (fake photo or address) while only existing online. Being online-only is fine; lying about a store isn't.
- **Fake owner/author profiles, e.g., AI content with invented "author" personas (AI photos or false bios) to make it look human-written.**
- **False claims of expertise**, e.g., claiming to be a medical professional when not.

**Deceptive design** examples:
- Ads disguised as MC, e.g., a page made to look like search results where every link is an ad; its "search box" just shows another page of ads.
- Ads disguised as navigation, e.g., a "directory" page that looks curated but links only to ads or similar pages on the same site.
- Deceptive buttons/links, e.g., a button that looks like it closes a pop-up but downloads an app.
- **Titles that mislead or have nothing to do with the content.** Visitors feel tricked.

No list will ever be complete because tactics keep evolving. If the true purpose isn't what it seems, or the page exists mainly to lure clicks on ads/monetized links/dubious downloads → Lowest.

### 6.4 Deliberately blocked or hidden MC (4.5.4, p. 38)

Examples that → Lowest:
- Ads that keep covering the MC while scrolling and are nearly impossible to close without clicking them.
- Pop-ups covering the MC that can only be closed by an action benefiting the site.
- Interstitials forcing downloads/clicks that don't help the visitor.
- Ads pushing the MC so far down that many people never see it.
- White-on-white text, or MC deliberately made hard for humans to read.

**Not reasons for Lowest:**
- **Logins or paywalls** on trustworthy sites (newspapers and magazines need subscriptions; banking and private profiles must be login-protected).
- MC inaccessible for unintended reasons (fails to load, unavailable in a country).
- **The mere presence of ads.**

### 6.5 Suspected malicious behavior (4.5.5, p. 38)

Lowest may be given on strong suspicion, even without proof:
- Strongly suspected scams.
- Asking for personal data without a legitimate reason (name, birthdate, address, bank account, ID numbers).
- Phishing for passwords to popular services.
- Links strongly suspected to be malware.
- Browser/antivirus malware warnings → assume malicious. (Not every browser warning is about malware, e.g., certificate prompts or content filters.)

## 7. Spammy pages (4.6, pp. 39–44)

**Background:** pages should exist for visitors' benefit. Pages built for the owner's gain without regard for visitors, then using deceptive techniques to rank, crowd out content made with effort and skill. The official reference is **Google Search Web Spam Policies**. Recognized spam technique → Lowest. (The full spam policies, including technical and link spam not detailed in SQRG, are summarized in `14-spam-policies.md`.)

### 7.1 Unclear purpose, no MC, very little/poor MC, no added value (4.6.1, p. 39)
Lowest if any is true:
- The purpose can't be understood because the MC is gibberish/unusable.
- Deliberately no MC.
- Deliberately so little MC that it achieves no purpose.
- Deliberately such poor MC that it achieves no purpose.
- **MC made with little or no effort, no originality, and no added value versus similar pages on the web.**

### 7.2 Hacked, defaced or spammed pages (4.6.2, p. 39)
- Sites altered without the owner's permission (unrelated content injected, MC changed) → Lowest because they fail their original purpose.
- **Spam comments**: unrelated comments to advertise or plant links, often by bots. Pages with many spam comments → Lowest.
- Responsible owners should regularly monitor and protect their sites.

### 7.3 Expired domain abuse (4.6.3, p. 40)
- **Definition:** buying a lapsed domain and reusing it mainly for the new owner's benefit with low-value content.
- The old domain often belonged to a trusted institution (government, charity, school). The new owner rides its reputation to rank. Content is usually heavily monetized, and visitors are confused.
- Examples: affiliate content on a former government agency site; commercial medical products on a former medical charity site; gambling content on what used to be a primary school's site.
- **How to detect:** the URL/site traits don't match the content (e.g., a school domain about payday loans or gambling). Confirm via the **Internet Archive** to see a drastic change.

### 7.4 Site reputation abuse (4.6.4, p. 41)
- **Definition:** third-party content placed on a host site **chiefly to exploit the ranking signals the host already has** (earned through its own first-party content), so the content ranks better than it would alone.
- Third party = an entity separate from the host: users, freelancers, white-label services, writers not directly employed by the host.
- **Third-party content alone isn't a violation.** The violation is the motive of riding the host's ranking signals.

| Counts as abuse | Does not |
|---|---|
| An education site hosting sponsored payday-loan reviews from a third party who distributes the same page to many sites | Wire services or press-release services |
| A medical site hosting an ad page about "best casinos" its readers wouldn't expect | News outlets carrying syndicated news from other outlets |
| A movie-review site hosting pages on buying followers, fortune-teller sites, or essay-writing services | Sites built for user content (forums, comment sections) |
| A news site hosting coupons from a white-label service mainly to exploit its reputation | Columns, opinion pieces, articles and other editorial work |
| An established site entering a new area mainly with freelance content because it will rank more easily there | Advertorials/native ads aimed directly at readers (e.g., promoted within the publication), not at manipulating rankings |
| | Properly treated affiliate links, or third-party ad units |
| | Coupons sourced directly from merchants |

### 7.5 Scaled content abuse (4.6.5, p. 42)
- A hallmark of spam sites: **lots of content, little effort/originality, no editing or manual curation.**
- **Definition:** many pages generated mainly for the owner's benefit rather than to help users, typically unoriginal content of very little value compared with similar pages, **however it's produced**.
- Examples:
  - Leaning on automation (AI or any other kind) as a cheap way to mass-produce pages with no added value.
  - Harvesting feeds, search results or other material to churn out many pages, including through automated rewrites (synonym spinning, translation, other obfuscation).
  - Stitching together content from different pages with no added value.
  - Creating many sites to hide the scale of production.
  - Creating many pages that make little sense to readers but contain search keywords.
- **Lowest regardless of method.** Raters needn't know whether AI was used: if, after looking at several pages on the site, scaled content abuse is strongly suspected → Lowest.

### 7.6 MC with little or no effort, originality and added value (4.6.6, pp. 42–43)
- **Lowest if all or nearly all of the MC** (text, images, audio, video) **is copied, paraphrased, embedded, auto/AI-generated, or reposted** from elsewhere with little or no effort, originality and added value. **Still Lowest even when the original source is credited.**
- "Little or no effort" means no effort **of any kind** (including building features), per the effort definition in 3.2.
- **Copied** = scraped from unaffiliated sites with no original content or added value. Images may be slightly cropped, videos altered, even re-recorded from a screen, to evade detection.
- **Licensed or syndicated content** (e.g., wire-service news) is **not** copied.
- **Paraphrased** = unoriginal content that exists elsewhere, reworded or restructured, manually or with automated tools.
- **Embedded** = legitimate, but Lowest if there's no meaningful original content or added value.
- **Reposts** on social media = legitimate, but Lowest with no added content, context or discussion.

**Forms of copied/paraphrased content:**
- Exact copies from an identifiable source (whole page, part of it, or several pages combined). Easiest to spot.
- **Copies with minimal alteration**: a few words or sentences swapped, find-and-replace, cropped images, split videos, to make tracing harder.
- Copies of dynamic sources (search results, news feeds). The original often can't be found, but it still counts as copied.
- Paraphrases of one or many sources by humans or AI. **Paraphrasing can add value**, e.g., an expert explaining a government policy in plain language.

**Key principles:**
- Copying, paraphrasing, embedding or reposting is **not automatically Lowest**. Lowest applies when **all or nearly all of the MC** is like that **with no effort, originality and added value**.
- **Using generative AI does not by itself determine the effort level or PQ rating.** AI can be used for high-quality content (e.g., original artwork made with real effort) or for Lowest content.

## 8. How to check for copied or paraphrased content (4.6.7, pp. 43–44)

1. Take a sentence/phrase from the page (pick one without odd punctuation or words that look swapped). Try several.
2. Search Google with quotes (exact match) and without (looser match).
3. Compare matching pages: is most of the MC the same? Which comes from an authoritative source known for original content? Which looks original? Which was published first (check the **Wayback Machine**)?

PDF example (p. 44): several sentences from a page about a classic fantasy film are searched with and without quotes. An old version of a Wikipedia article in the Wayback Machine proves the text was copied. Searching against the current Wikipedia article finds nothing, showing that proving a copy can be hard.

**Signs of paraphrased content** (harder to spot):
- Only common knowledge or widely known facts.
- Heavy overlap with established sources such as Wikipedia or reference sites.
- Appears to summarize one specific page (a forum thread, a news article) with no added value.
- Traces of AI tools, e.g., a leftover chatbot sentence where the model refers to itself as an AI language model.

## 9. Implications for content creators

**[Application]**
- **Test added value before publishing:** what's on this page that isn't in the top 10 results? If the answer is "nothing", the page risks falling into 4.6.1/4.6.6 territory.
- **Mass production (programmatic SEO, AI articles at volume):** each page needs unique data, curation and human editing. Volume without added value is the definition of scaled content abuse, whatever the tool.
- **Don't create fictional author personas** or AI-generated author photos. This is explicitly named as deceptive info.
- **Don't claim "we tested it"** without real testing. This is explicitly named as deceptive purpose.
- **Titles must match content.** A misleading clickbait title can drop a page to Lowest even if the body is ordinary.
- **Third-party content** (guest posts, coupons, sponsored articles, freelance content in new topics): make sure it fits the site and its readers, rather than just riding domain authority.
- **Buying old domains:** don't fill them with topics unrelated to the domain's history.
- **Ads and pop-ups:** never let them cover the MC or be hard to close, especially on mobile.
- **Comment moderation** and site security (comment spam, hacking) are the owner's responsibility.
- **YMYL:** a single claim that contradicts expert consensus and could cause harm can be enough for Lowest.
