## 1. What Page Quality rating is for (p. 9)

The core of PQ: **how well a page achieves its purpose.** Raters assess the landing page as well as its parent site.

## 2. Key definitions (2.1, pp. 9–10)

- **User**: anyone searching for information or trying to get a task done online, from any background (age, gender, religion, politics, etc.).
- **Webpage**: no longer just text and links; also media (images, video) and functionality (shopping, calculators, games, email).
- **Website**: a set of pages owned by one entity. Large sections/hosts with their own purpose (e.g., the finance or sports subdomain of a big portal) may be treated as websites in their own right.
- **Homepage**: the site's main page, usually reached via the logo or a "home" link.
- **Website owner**: whoever is responsible for the site.
- **Content creator**: the person or entity that made the content on the page.
- **Generative AI**: ML models that produce new content (text, images, music, code). SQRG frames it as a **tool** that can help content creation or be misused. AI is not automatically good or bad.

## 3. Page purpose (2.2, pp. 10–11)

- Every page exists for one or more purposes. Most aim to help people (beneficial purpose). Some exist mainly to earn money while barely trying to help. Some are even built to cause harm.
- **Step one is understanding the purpose**, because:
  - PQ measures how well that purpose is achieved;
  - the purpose determines which criteria matter;
  - a page not created to help people may deserve **Lowest**.
- **No page type is inherently higher quality.** An encyclopedia page is not automatically better than a humor page. Every type (stores, news, forums, video, error pages, PDFs, images, gossip, humor, homepages) has both top and bottom quality examples.
- Examples of beneficial purposes: sharing information; sharing personal experience, perspective or feelings; sharing photos/video; showing a talent; expressing an opinion; entertaining; offering products/services; hosting Q&A; sharing files or software; supporting a charity or cause.
- Purpose mapping examples: news homepage → informing about current events; product page → selling or describing a product; video page → sharing a funny video; currency converter → calculating exchange values.
- A purpose that isn't obvious at first (p. 11): a humor site where users post photos of objects with mouths drawn on them. Odd at first glance, but its purpose is humor/artistic expression, so it is beneficial. The site explains itself on its FAQ page even though its About page is unhelpful.

## 4. YMYL topics (2.3, pp. 11–13)

**Definition:** topics where poor content carries real risk: it could seriously damage people's health, money or safety, or the well-being of society.

**Who can be affected:** (1) the person reading/using the content; (2) other people affected by the reader's actions; (3) groups or society at large.

**Two routes to YMYL:**
1. **The topic itself is harmful**, e.g., self-harm, crime, violent extremism.
2. **The topic can cause harm if the content is inaccurate**, e.g., heart attack symptoms, how to invest, what to do in an earthquake, who can vote, driver's license requirements. Here, even small inaccuracies can have serious consequences.

**Four YMYL categories:**

| Category | Scope |
|---|---|
| Health or Safety | Mental, physical, emotional health; physical and online safety |
| Financial Security | Whether someone can keep providing for themselves and their household |
| Government, Civics & Society | Impact on groups of people, public-interest issues, trust in public institutions, election and voting info |
| Other | Other topics that could hurt people or harm societal well-being |

**Important:** a topic is not YMYL just because one can imagine a malicious page about it (e.g., a page on the science of rainbows that bundles a virus). What counts is **the potential impact of the topic itself**.

**YMYL is a spectrum:** clearly YMYL → in between → clearly not. Most topics are not YMYL. Pages on **clearly YMYL** topics get the strictest PQ scrutiny.

**Calibration matrix (condensed from the table on p. 12):**

| Content type | Clearly YMYL | Maybe YMYL | Not / unlikely YMYL |
|---|---|---|---|
| Information | Tsunami evacuation routes | Weather forecast | Music award winners |
| Advice on an activity | When to go to the ER | How often to replace a toothbrush | How often to wash jeans |
| Personal opinion | Opinion that a racial group is inferior (used to justify violence) | Opinion that a type of exercise is inferior | Opinion that a rock band is inferior |
| News | Ongoing violence | A car accident | A local high-school basketball game |
| Social media post | A dangerous challenge linked to deaths | A hot-sauce challenge | A music video |
| Shopping & reviews | Buying prescription drugs | A car review | Buying pencils |

Guiding questions by type: information → could inaccurate info cause significant harm? Advice → could poor advice cause significant harm? Opinion → what impact on others and society? News → significant impact on society (including elections and trust in institutions)? Social media → could it hurt individuals or damage society if widely shared? Shopping → could the product itself cause harm?

**Two quick tests (p. 13):**
1. Would a cautious person turn to experts or very trusted sources to stay safe? Could even small inaccuracies cause harm? → If yes, likely YMYL.
2. Would most people be content just casually asking friends about it? → If yes, likely not YMYL.

## 5. Parts of a page: MC, SC, Ads (2.4, pp. 13–15)

All page content falls into one of three categories. Raters are told to click around, including content behind tabs.

**Main Content (MC)**: the part that **directly** serves the page's purpose. Can be text, images, video, features (calculators, games), and user-generated content (reviews, comments, articles). Content behind tabs (e.g., reviews, shipping, safety info on a product page) can be MC.
- **The page title is part of the MC.** A descriptive title that summarizes the content helps people decide which pages to visit.

**Supplementary Content (SC)**: supports the user experience but doesn't directly achieve the purpose. The most common type: navigation to other parts of the site. SC can strengthen a page or distract from it.

**Advertisements/Monetization (Ads)**: content/links shown to make money, including **any monetized link (e.g., affiliate)**.
- **Having ads, or not having them, doesn't on its own push a rating up or down.** Many sites couldn't exist without monetization.
- The site owner is **still responsible** for the quality of ads shown, even if they come from an ad network.
- Monetization on mobile can be subtler than on desktop.

**Practical approach:** identify the MC first, then the Ads; treat the rest as SC. There's no need to classify every small element. User reviews/comments can be MC on one page and SC on another, depending on purpose.

**About the images in this section:** the visual examples in the PDF (pp. 13–15) are links to screenshots highlighting MC in yellow, SC in blue and Ads in red, for news pages (homepage and article), a store product page (shown with no ads), a blog page, a search engine homepage, a bank login page, and a currency converter. The screenshots themselves are not embedded in the PDF.

## 6. Understanding the website (2.5, pp. 15–18)

**Principle:** pages often make more sense as part of their site. Find out who is responsible for the site and who created the content, then look for **reputation from independent sources**. If what a site says about itself conflicts with credible independent sources, **trust the independent sources**.

**Finding the homepage (2.5.1):** click the site's logo/name; if there's no navigation, trim the URL back to the domain. With two homepage candidates (e.g., subdomain vs main domain), use whichever tells you most about the page being rated.
- Image examples (p. 16): a screenshot of a knife-set product page on a kitchenware retailer with a red box around the top-center logo leading to the homepage. Second: a medical school's "facts and figures" page, whose top-left logo leads to the school's own homepage; that one is used rather than the parent university's homepage.

**Who is responsible and who created the content (2.5.2):** every page should make clear (1) who is responsible for the website and (2) who created the page's content. On forums/social media an alias or username is an acceptable identity. Sites usually disclose this because they want recognition, protect copyright, build buyer trust, etc.

**Primary content creator by page type (condensed from the table on p. 17):**

| Situation | Examples | Primary creator |
|---|---|---|
| Site owner made the page and most of the MC | Business homepage, intro page of a personal site, online store product page | The website itself (responsibility stays with the owner even if the build was outsourced). User reviews can be significant as long as the site actively maintains the page. |
| Site owner made the page; MC by named authors | Newspaper editorial-board opinion, magazine article by a journalist, journal paper | The listed authors (individuals or organizations). The site still decides what gets published and is responsible. |
| Posts from a single social media account | Social post, local business profile, video channel | The account holder posting the MC. Creator info may be on the profile page. |
| Many users in discussion | Forum thread, Q&A page, search results on a social platform | The posters. No single primary creator; identities may be usernames only. |

**About, contact and customer service (2.5.3):**
- How much contact info is needed **depends on the site type**.
- For sites that **handle money** (stores, banks, credit cards), contact and customer service info is **extremely important**.
- Shopping sites get special checks: contact info plus **payment, exchange and return policies** (often under customer service).
- A humor site doesn't need the contact detail expected of an online bank.
- Legitimate anonymity exists: personal sites needn't show a home address; UGC sites may use aliases only.
- Conventions vary by country/locale and by social platform.

## 7. Implications for content creators

**[Application]**
- Define **one main purpose** per page before writing. Everything is judged against it.
- The **H1 and title** must be descriptive and honestly summarize the content. The title counts as MC.
- Put the **MC front and center**. Ads and affiliate links are fine, but their quality and placement are the site's responsibility.
- Show **author identity** (name, role, relevant credentials) and **publisher identity** (About, Contact) appropriate to the site type.
- E-commerce: contact, customer service and payment/shipping/return policies must be easy to find.
- YMYL topics: apply the strictest standards of accuracy and sourcing (see `02-pq-reputation-eeat.md`).
- Indonesian local conventions (examples): news outlets usually have an editorial-team (Redaksi) page and cyber-media reporting guidelines; online stores usually show a WhatsApp/CS number, address and return policy. Follow what readers in the locale expect.
