## 1. The three PQ steps and the scale (3.0, p. 19)

How a rater thinks, in order:
1. **Judge the true purpose.** Harmful, or deceptive about its real purpose → **Lowest**.
2. **Judge the potential for harm.** Harmful to people/society, untrustworthy, or spammy → **Lowest**.
3. **If it passes both**, the rating depends on **how well the page fulfills its purpose**.

Note: harmful pages can look "official", "expert" or "authoritative", including pages that imitate scientific papers or encyclopedias. **Every** page is checked for harm, including on government, academic, big retail and charity sites.

**Scale (slider on p. 19):** Lowest · Low · Medium · High · Highest, with in-between values Lowest+, Low+, Medium+, High+ (each meaning half a step up).
- Telling Lowest from Low is the hardest part, so that guidance is the longest.
- High and Highest are relatively easier to recognize.
- Medium pages are the most common on the web.

## 2. PQ considerations (3.1, pp. 19–20)

| Consideration | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Page purpose | Harmful or deceptive purpose (including about who's responsible) → Lowest |
| Potential for harm | Harmful, untrustworthy or spammy → Lowest. Otherwise, judge how well the purpose is met |
| Topic and degree of YMYL | Sets the standard; YMYL is stricter |
| Website type | Different expectations: small hobby site vs big corporation; sites with transactions/personal data vs none; volunteer content vs professional |
| Info from the site and creator | Not always trustworthy, but an important starting point |
| MC quality | How satisfying it is and how well it serves the purpose |
| Page title | Part of the MC; descriptive titles help people choose |
| Role of Ads and SC | How they shape the experience. Ads existing is not itself a consideration |
| Reputation of site and creator | How others see them and who's behind them |
| Trust of the page | How trustworthy; Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness help assess Trust |

These considerations **overlap by design**. Example: while assessing MC you spot factual errors → Trust drops. While researching reputation you find proof of the author's expertise → Trust rises.

## 3. Main Content quality (3.2, p. 21)

The unifying theme: **how far the MC lets the page achieve its purpose and gives a satisfying experience.** For most pages, four things:

- **Effort:** how actively a human worked to create satisfying content. Effort can be direct (personally translating a poem) or indirect (building systems/features, e.g., a machine-translation service for users).
  - **Not effort:** auto-generating thousands of pages by running existing free content through translation software **with no oversight, curation or human touch**.
  - On social media/forums, effort shows in participation level and depth of discussion. Many people's contributions can add up to a lot of effort.
- **Originality:** how unique the content is and whether it's available elsewhere. If similar content exists, is this page the original source?
- **Talent or skill:** is the creator skilled enough to give a satisfying experience?
- **Accuracy:** factually correct for informational pages. For YMYL: accurate **and in line with settled expert consensus**.

**Weighting depends on context:** medical advice → expert consensus; how-to video → skill; art → talent and originality. A short social video needs less effort than a professional documentary, but both must be enough for their purpose.

**How to check:** spend a few minutes. Read the article, watch the video, try the calculator, play the game. On a product page, try adding an item to the cart. Imagine being someone very interested in the topic: how satisfied would they be?

High- and low-quality content exists in **every format and length**, and on **every type of site**, from small personal blogs to large corporations.

## 4. Reputation of website and creators (3.3, pp. 22–25)

**General principles (3.3):**
- Reputation research is **required on every PQ task**. Content can look great on the surface while reputation research exposes scams.
- If the site isn't the main creator of the MC, research the creator too.
- Judge reputation **for the page's topic**: a site can be a go-to for funny videos yet untrustworthy for financial information.
- Reputation also shows what a site/creator is known for (a newspaper for investigative journalism, a satire site for humor, a food blogger for tasty recipes).
- If one entity works across many platforms (own site, YouTube, social, TV), research **the entity behind them**.

**Website reputation (3.3.1):**
- Based on real users' experience and expert opinion; covers the website **and** the real organization it represents.
- **Be skeptical of what a site says about itself**, especially with a conflict of interest.
- Good sources: independent reviews, references, expert recommendations, news articles, Wikipedia, magazine articles, forum discussions, ratings from independent organizations. Prefer writing by people over machine-compiled statistics.
- **For YMYL:** judge reputation by what **experts in the field** say. Recommendations from expert sources (e.g., professional associations) count as strong signs of a good reputation.
- For news sites: prestigious journalism awards or a track record of quality original reporting are strong evidence.
- **Traffic data (e.g., Similarweb) is not reputation evidence.** Ignore it.

**Customer reviews (3.3.2):**
- Many detailed, trustworthy positive reviews → evidence of positive reputation for a store/business.
- Interpret with care: (1) treat both glowing and negative reviews with caution, since anyone can write them, including owners or paid writers; (2) gather as many as possible, since a few negatives are normal, especially for big businesses; (3) **read the content**, not just star counts. Credible reports of fraud = very negative reputation. One rude cashier or one late parcel ≠ negative reputation.

**How to search for reputation (3.3.3):**
1. Identify the site's homepage (and the creator, if different).
2. Search with operators that exclude the site itself, e.g., `[brandname -site:domain.com]`, `["domain.com" -site:domain.com]`, `[brandname reviews -site:domain.com]`, `["domain.com" reviews -site:domain.com]`.
3. Look for articles, expert recommendations and other credible info, e.g., `[brandname site:en.wikipedia.org]`. The brand's own official social accounts are **not** independent sources. Articles flagged as disputed/outdated signal that more research is needed.
4. Make sure the sources fit the judgment: reputation for a YMYL topic should come from sources expert in that topic.

**Reputation examples (condensed from the table on p. 24):**
- Positive: the official US free-credit-report site (the only federally mandated and authorized source); a hospital site rated among the best in the US (confirmed by credible news), so its medical info can be trusted; a newspaper site that has won several prestigious awards; the Linux kernel source repository, the main reference for developers.
- Very negative: a children's play-equipment store that looks tidy, but is rated very poorly on a business-review platform, news coverage of financial fraud, and many reports of people paying and receiving nothing.
- Very negative/malicious: an eyewear seller involved in criminal behavior, including physically threatening customers.
- Very negative: an organization claiming to serve hospitalized veterans, with many news and charity-watchdog articles reporting fraud and mismanaged funds.

**Creator reputation (3.3.4):**
- Sources: biographical articles and online discussions.
- Professional creators (journalists, scientists, academics) usually have a formal trail: degrees, peer validation, expert co-authors, citations, relevant work history.
- Influencers: from biographies and news to comments by peers in their field.
- Ordinary people on forums/social media: informal reputation, e.g., other people's comments about them on the page.

**When no reputation info exists (3.3.5):**
- Big sites and well-known creators should have a reputation trail.
- Small sites, local businesses, communities, or ordinary people using aliases often don't. **That's neither a good nor a bad sign.**
- If no reputation info is found, look harder at the other PQ considerations, especially for YMYL.

## 5. E-E-A-T (3.4, pp. 26–27)

**Diagram (p. 26):** three overlapping circles (Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness) with **Trust in the center**. Trust is the most important member.

**Trust:** how **accurate, honest, safe and reliable** the page is. The kind and amount of Trust depends on the page:
- Online store → secure payments and reliable customer service.
- Product review → honest, written to help people decide, not purely to sell.
- YMYL informational page → must be accurate to prevent harm.
- Non-YMYL social post for entertainment → doesn't need that level of Trust.

**The three supports of Trust:**
- **Experience:** does the creator have first-hand or lived experience relevant to the topic? Example: a review by someone who actually used the product vs a "review" by someone who never did.
- **Expertise:** does the creator have the needed knowledge/skill? Example: home rewiring advice from a skilled electrician vs from an antique-house enthusiast who doesn't understand wiring.
- **Authoritativeness:** is the creator/site known as the go-to source for the topic? Most topics have no single official source, but when one exists it's usually the most reliable. Examples: a local shop's social profile for today's deals; the official government site for passport renewal.

The three can overlap (expertise can grow from years of experience), and the relevant mix differs per topic. Ask: **given this page's purpose, type and topic, what would make its creator worth trusting?**

**Why Trust matters most:** an untrustworthy page has low E-E-A-T however experienced or expert it seems. A highly skilled scammer is still a scammer.

**Three sources of E-E-A-T evidence:**
1. **What the site/creator says about itself** (About, profile): a starting point.
2. **What others say** (independent reviews, references, news): is there independent evidence of experience/expertise/authority, or of dishonesty?
3. **What's visible on the page** (MC, reviews, comments): expertise sometimes shows directly in the content. Example: a hairstylist's video of them styling hair, plus viewer comments praising their skill.

**Conflict of interest:** reviews from owners sharing their experience can be very valuable. "Reviews" by the manufacturer itself or by an influencer paid to promote are less trustworthy.

**Trust is broader than E-E-A:** e.g., customer service info for online stores, peer-reviewed publications for academic authors. A page untrustworthy for any reason = low E-E-A-T.

## 6. YMYL: Experience or Expertise? (3.4.1, p. 28)

- If a YMYL page's purpose is to **give information or advice**, a **high level of expertise** is often needed.
- If its purpose is **sharing lived experience** (e.g., coping with hard times), first-hand experience can be highly valuable. Facts from experts alone don't meet the need of someone looking for support or inspiration.
- Condition: experience-based content must still be **trustworthy, safe, and consistent with expert consensus**.

| YMYL topic | Suits personal experience | Best left to experts |
|---|---|---|
| Trouble sleeping while pregnant (Health) | Safe, non-medical tips from people who've been through it, e.g., safe pillow positions late in pregnancy | Which sleep medications are safe during pregnancy |
| Liver cancer treatment (Health) | A sincere forum discussion about coping with treatment | Treatment options and life expectancy for each |
| Filling in tax forms (Financial) | A non-expert's humorous video about the frustration of doing taxes | How to fill in the tax forms |
| Retirement savings (Financial) | Reviews of retirement-saving services from actual users | How much to save, which assets, how much is needed to retire at a given age |
| How to vote (Society) | A citizen's post on why they think voting in local elections matters | Who is eligible to vote and how to register |

## 7. Implications for content creators

**[Application]**
- **Make effort visible.** Add what an automated summary can't: your own data, original photos/process, testing, case studies, local examples, analysis.
- **Mass AI or translated content without curation doesn't count as effort.** If you use AI, ensure research, verification and human value-add.
- **YMYL:** match expert consensus (Indonesia's Ministry of Health, OJK, Bank Indonesia, KPU, journals, etc.), cite sources, and involve expert reviewers when giving advice.
- **Separate experience formats from expert-advice formats.** "My experience" articles are fine on YMYL topics, as long as they don't give medical/financial advice that should come from experts.
- **Build E-E-A-T evidence others can verify:** author pages, credentials, off-site profiles, media coverage, association recommendations. One-sided claims on an About page aren't enough.
- **Reviews/affiliate:** show proof of direct use and disclose commercial relationships. Conflicts of interest lower Trust.
- **Audit your own brand's reputation** with the same `-site:` operators raters use.
